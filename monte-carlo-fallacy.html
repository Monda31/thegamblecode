The Monte Carlo fallacy: why roulette streaks do not "even out"

After six blacks in a row, is red "due"? The wheel does not know.

What happened in 1913

The story is told in many books on probability: in August 1913, at the Monte Carlo Casino, black came up 26 times in a row. As the streak grew, players piled onto red, convinced it had to come. Many lost large sums. The wheel had no memory, and each spin was still an independent event.

The maths

On a European wheel black has probability 18/37, about 48.6%. That number is the same on every spin, whatever happened before. After six blacks the chance of a seventh black is still 48.6%, and so is the chance of red. Only zero (2.7%) changes the total.

The chance of 26 blacks in a row starting from a given spin is (18/37)26, about 1 in 137 million. That is extremely rare, but a casino spins wheels thousands of times a day for years, so rare runs do happen somewhere. It does not tell you anything about the next spin.

Over many spins, runs like these show up regularly. Seeing one does not mean the wheel is broken, and it does not mean the opposite colour is coming.

What this means for betting plans

Any plan that says "bet more because the other side is due" is built on this fallacy. A plan can still be useful if it caps your risk and you treat the game as entertainment, but it cannot use streaks to beat the house edge. See how the house edge works.

Watch for the feeling. "It has to turn now" is the thought that leads people to raise stakes after losses. A fixed stop-loss is the antidote.
