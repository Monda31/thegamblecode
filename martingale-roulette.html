Does the Martingale system work in roulette?

It wins small amounts often and loses large amounts rarely. Over time that is not an advantage.

The idea

You bet on an even-money outcome (red/black, odd/even) and double the stake after each loss. When you finally win, you recover all previous losses plus one base unit of profit.

What a real bankroll can take

Say you start with 1,000 and bet 10. Six losses in a row costs 630 and the next bet would be 640, more than the 370 you have left. So the plan stops after six consecutive losses. On a European wheel the chance of six losses in a row starting from a given spin is 1.8%, about 1 in 55. That is small per attempt, but you make many attempts.

A simulation

We simulated 200 spins on a European wheel, starting with 1,000 and a base bet of 10, doubling after each loss and returning to 10 after a win, and stopping when the next bet cannot be placed (20,000 runs). About 69% of runs hit a streak the bankroll could not cover. The average final balance was about 898 out of 1,000, which is a loss. These numbers come from our own simulation, not a published study, so treat them as an illustration.

Why it cannot work

  • Every spin has a negative expected value (-2.7% on a European wheel). Doubling changes the shape of the results, not the average.
  • Tables have a maximum bet, which can block the next doubling even if you have the money.
  • The wins are tiny (one base unit per cycle) and the losses are big.
Bottom line. If you use a progression, cap it. Decide the maximum number of steps before you start and treat reaching it as a loss you have already accepted. Our guides always state the maximum risk per attack up front.
